After I wrote about my on-again-off-again love affair with AI, a few of you asked the obvious follow-up question: okay, but how do you use it responsibly? Fair question. "Use it thoughtfully" is easy to say and a lot harder to put into practice on a Tuesday afternoon when you just need the newsletter done.

So here's what "responsible" actually looks like in my day-to-day - broken into the two things that worry people most: the planet, and their data.

## Reducing the environmental footprint

Those that know me, know that I am passionate about environmental sustainability. And this is where I find my use of AI most conflicting.

Every AI request draws real power from a real data centre somewhere, so the goal is not to stop using the tools; it is to use them with a bit more intention. So here are a few tips that I have put together to help you be a good kind of keyboard warrior:

- **Ask better:** A clear, specific prompt gets you a usable answer in one go instead of five back-and-forth attempts, or with your AI bot coming back to you with questions. Fewer requests = less energy.
- **Reuse before you regenerate:** This is where using the Projects function (if you are like me and use Claude) is a life - and planet - saver. If a past output is 80% right, edit it rather than asking the tool to start again from scratch.
- **Batch similar tasks:** In a marketing context, this could be writing your blog, your e-newsletter and your monthly socials in one hit, instead of running them one at a time throughout the day. It is small but it is a lot more efficient.
- **Choose the right-sized tool for the job:** Not every task needs the biggest, most powerful model available. A lighter tool for a simple task uses meaningfully less energy.
- **Conscious AI user:** Support providers who are transparent about their energy use and are investing in renewables. Where you spend your subscription is a small vote for how the industry builds itself. Hint: I have switched from Google to Ecosia as my AI search engine. The same outputs as Google but the European-based Ecosia commits to reforestation.
- **AI is not the only option:** Sometimes the most responsible move is simply doing the quick task yourself. Or, you could use that old dinosaur called "The Internet" and go old school with a quick search.

## Keeping your data safe

This is a biggie. It is the one that should genuinely change how you use these tools day to day, especially in a small business where you are often handling other people's information too.

- **Never paste anything identifiable into a public AI tool:** Client names, contact details, financials, health information, anything you would not want screenshotted and shared. If you would not post it publicly, do not prompt it.
- **Get cozy with the privacy settings, not just the privacy policy:** Most major tools now let you turn off "use my data to train your model" - find that toggle and switch it on your side.
- **Separate your business tools from your personal ones:** This is one that I adopted early. A properly licensed business or team account usually comes with stronger data protections than a free personal login. In my opinion, it is worth the additional cost.
- **Anonymise before you generate:** Swap real names and figures for placeholders when you are drafting and drop the real details back in yourself afterwards.
- **Check where your data is stored and for how long:** If you cannot get a straight answer, that's information too.
- **Check EVERYTHING:** This is probably the biggest one for me. I love how AI has made my life easier but I learned the hard way - AI needs a human eye for accuracy. It is also your last line of defence against sensitive information slipping through. Absolutely nothing goes public unless I am personally comfortable with it.

None of this requires you to be a tech expert. It just requires the same instinct you would bring to any other business decision: know what you are using, know what it costs, and know what you are trusting it with.

That's really the heart of responsible AI use for a small business. It is not about swearing off the tools that are genuinely levelling the playing field for us. It is about retaining the human in charge of them. Sure, be curious, but also a little cautious, and never so dazzled by the output that we forget to ask the obvious questions.

Used well, AI still gives small businesses something remarkable: big brand output, without the big brand budget. We just have to keep our hands on the wheel.
